# Book Co-Author

## Overview

Most thought-leadership books fail because they're a portfolio of opinions, not a developed argument. The author's voice gets lost in the editorial polish. This skill keeps the author's specific frame and language while imposing structural rigor.

## When to Use

- Drafting a book chapter from voice notes / interviews / fragments
- Building a chapter outline from a thesis statement
- Editing for voice consistency across drafts written by multiple hands
- Developing the author's positioning ("the one frame they're known for")
- Structuring a long-form argument that needs to hold across 200+ pages

## Iron Law

```
PRESERVE THE AUTHOR'S SPECIFIC WORDS. Their odd phrasings, recurring
images, and signature analogies are what readers will quote and
remember. Editorial smoothing kills the voice that justified the book.

EVERY CHAPTER HAS ONE LOAD-BEARING IDEA. If the chapter summary needs
"and" or "also," split or cut. A chapter is a unit of argument, not a
catch-all for related thoughts.
```

## Checklist (any chapter)

1. **One-sentence thesis** for the chapter: "By the end, the reader will believe ___." → check: written at the top of the draft.
2. **Hook in first 150 words** — a story, a counterintuitive claim, a question the reader's been quietly carrying. → check: cut everything above the hook.
3. **Argument arc** — claim → tension → evidence (story or data) → reframe → implication. → check: each section maps to one of these.
4. **The author's recurring images / metaphors / signature phrases** survive editing. → check: tracked in a glossary; not silently smoothed away.
5. **One memorable line per chapter** — quotable, sticky, lifts out of context. → check: identified and protected through final pass.
6. **Concrete evidence** — at least one specific story, customer, or number per claim. No floating abstractions.
7. **Ends with a forward door** — sets up the next chapter or returns to the book's thesis. → check: reader has a reason to turn the page.

## Checklist (whole book)

1. **Thesis in 20 words** — the one idea the book delivers. → check: a reader can repeat it after reading the back-cover.
2. **Structure**: Why → What → How (or Problem → Frame → Application). → check: each chapter belongs in one of these three sections.
3. **Audience defined narrowly** — better to delight 10,000 specific readers than bore 100,000 general. → check: persona written, not "leaders" or "builders" generically.
4. **The author's frame** named clearly — the one diagram or phrase others will reference. → check: it appears in chapter 1 and recurs.
5. **Cut everything that's true but doesn't serve the thesis.** → check: a chapter that doesn't advance the argument is a blog post; release it as one.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Smoothing voice into generic management prose.** "Leverage synergies" is everyone; the author's "let's be honest, this was a dumpster fire" is no one else.
- **Quotes from famous people the author didn't actually engage with.** Reads as filler. The author's own experience is the differentiator.
- **Every chapter a how-to with bullet points.** Long-form needs prose density that earns the page count.
- **Disclaimers and caveats stacked in the front.** Trust the reader; address objections in line.
- **Ending with "in conclusion".** End with the most memorable image of the chapter.
- **Acronym soup**. If the author uses an acronym, define it once; if it's clever enough to repeat, define it well.
